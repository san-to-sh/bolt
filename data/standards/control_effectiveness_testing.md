# Synthetic Audit Standard — Control Operating-Effectiveness Testing

> **Synthetic summary for demo purposes — not an actual standard.**

## S1. Design Effectiveness vs. Operating Effectiveness

**Design effectiveness** asks whether a control, as designed, is capable of preventing or detecting a misstatement or policy violation if it operates as intended. Evaluating design effectiveness involves understanding the control's objective, the risk it addresses, and whether its mechanism logically addresses that risk.

**Operating effectiveness** asks whether the control actually operated as designed, consistently, throughout the period under examination. A control may be well-designed but ineffective if it was not applied, was applied inconsistently, or was overridden.

Testing operating effectiveness requires examining evidence that the control was performed — approvals, sign-offs, system logs, exception reports — across a representative set of occurrences during the period.

## S2. Deviation Rate and Tolerable Rate

A **deviation** is an instance where the control did not operate as designed. The **deviation rate** is the number of deviations observed divided by the number of items tested.

The **tolerable deviation rate** is the maximum rate of deviation the auditor is willing to accept while still concluding that the control is operating effectively. This rate is set based on the auditor's judgment, considering the importance of the control and the risk of the assertion being tested.

If the observed deviation rate exceeds the tolerable rate, the auditor cannot conclude that the control operated effectively during the period and must consider the implications for the audit approach.

## S3. Sample Size and Confidence

Larger samples provide greater confidence in the conclusion. The appropriate sample size depends on the expected deviation rate, the tolerable deviation rate, and the desired confidence level. When the full population is small enough to test completely, sampling is unnecessary and the deviation rate is exact.

## S4. Evaluating Results

When deviations are found, the auditor considers: the nature and cause of the deviation, whether it is an isolated event or indicative of a systemic weakness, whether compensating controls exist, and whether the deviation resulted in an actual misstatement. The auditor exercises professional judgment in reaching a conclusion.
